If You're Not First, You're Last is Grant Cardone's argument that selling is the activity every business depends on, and that a downturn is the best moment to take ground from competitors. Where most advice about a weak economy counsels caution, the book counsels offence: contact more people, follow up harder, serve better and avoid discounting your way out of trouble.

Judging from the verified table of contents, the chapters move from who to sell to (your personal network, past clients, the unsold, existing accounts), through how to sell (the call, service, price, the second sale, the value-added proposition, marketing, repackaging), to the habits underneath (a protected schedule, attitude, personal finances, a core skill, and the "unreasonable" attitude).

Claude's judgment, not a verified fact: from its structure and how it is described, it reads as energetic, repetitive by design and motivational, closer to a sales manager's pep talk than a research text. That is both its appeal and its limit.

Introduction
I
Introduction
›
Cardone opens with the premise that most people respond to a tough economy by shrinking: cutting costs, pulling back on selling, and waiting for conditions to improve. He argues the opposite instinct is the profitable one, and that the book is a field manual for going on offence.
Selling harder in a downturn is not reckless; waiting is.
Key idea: Offence over defence
1
Four Responses to Economic Contractions
›
The contents list this chapter's sections as four named responses: the Cheerleader, Old School, Quitter and Advance and Conquer. Cardone sets the first three against the fourth and argues for the last: committing to take market share while competitors hesitate. He makes the case that contractions are when leaders are made and when weak competitors leave customers up for grabs.

The chapter sets the tone for everything that follows: the economy is a condition, not an excuse, and the only variable fully under your control is how hard and how smartly you sell.
The market you are in is not the problem; your response to it is the variable.
Key concept: Four responses, one of which is Advance and Conquer
2
Power Base Reactivation
›
Your power base is the network of people who already know, like, and trust you: friends, family, former colleagues, acquaintances, vendors. Cardone argues most salespeople ignore this group because it feels awkward to sell to people they know, yet it is the fastest, cheapest source of new business.

He pushes readers to build the list, contact everyone on it, and ask directly for business or introductions rather than hinting.
Reviewer's own illustration: a consultant lists 40 former colleagues and sends each a two-line note naming one problem she now solves, asking for either work or an introduction.
Your fastest revenue is usually sitting in your own contacts list.
Key tool: The Power Base list
3
Past Client Reactivation
›
Past clients have already bought from you once, which makes them the warmest prospects you have. Cardone calls out the common failure of treating a closed sale as the end of the relationship, then wondering where repeat business went.

The chapter is a push to dig up every dormant account, call them, find out what has changed, and re-engage with a specific offer rather than a vague "just checking in."
Reviewer's own illustration: a plumbing firm sorts its customer list by last job date and calls the oldest 20 with a seasonal service offer instead of a generic check-in.
A client you have already sold once costs far less to sell again than a stranger.
Key tool: Dormant-account reactivation calls
4
The Most Effective Call to Advance and Conquer
›
From the title and themes, this is the book's tactical chapter on the sales call, which Cardone treats as a core unit of selling activity. The goal of the call is to move the prospect to the next concrete step, not just have a pleasant conversation.

Expect emphasis on purpose, persistence and asking for the next step directly (my reading of the theme, not a checked detail).
Every call should end with a clear next step, not a pleasant goodbye.
Key practice: Purposeful, high-volume calling
5
Converting the Unsold
›
Most of the pipeline is made of people who said no, not yet, or nothing at all. Cardone argues that an unsold prospect is not a dead prospect: it is an unfinished sale. The chapter title points to following up on prospects who did not buy first time.

The underlying belief is that a no usually means the prospect lacks information, urgency, or confidence, all of which a persistent, helpful seller can address.
Reviewer's own illustration: a software rep reopens every quote from the last quarter that went silent and emails each with one new fact, such as a changed price or feature.
"No" is usually a timing or information problem, not a verdict.
Key concept: Treat the unsold as unfinished sales
6
Multiply through Existing Clients
›
Existing customers can be multiplied: through additional purchases, wider use inside their organization, and referrals to people like them. Cardone argues that happy clients are under-asked, and that most sellers leave large amounts of money on the table by not requesting introductions.

The chapter encourages a deliberate system for asking existing clients what else they need and who else they know.
Your best prospecting channel is a satisfied client who has been asked.
Key practice: Systematic referral and expansion asks
7
Delivering at "Wow" Levels
›
Service is a sales tool. Cardone argues that merely satisfying customers is not enough in a contracting market, and that exceeding expectations is what generates repeat purchases, referrals, and pricing power.

He frames the wow level as something you design on purpose: finding small, visible ways to over-deliver so clients talk about you without prompting.
Satisfied customers leave quietly; wowed customers recruit for you.
Key concept: Deliberate over-delivery
8
The Importance of Price
›
Cardone tackles the reflex to cut price when sales slow. He argues discounting is often a symptom of weak selling, and that price is rarely the only factor buyers weigh when the seller has built enough value and trust.

The chapter pushes sellers to hold their price, explain the value behind it, and treat price objections as a prompt to sell more, not to give more away.
Reviewer's own illustration: before offering a 10% discount, the seller lists three benefits the buyer has not yet heard and offers those first.
Cutting price is the easiest move and often the most expensive one.
Key idea: Defend price by building value
9
Activate Second Sale to Boost Profits
›
After the first sale comes the second: the add-on, upgrade, or companion product the client did not know they needed. Cardone argues that the best moment to sell more is right after a customer has said yes, when trust and momentum are highest.

He encourages sellers to plan the second sale before the first closes, so it is part of the process rather than an afterthought.
Reviewer's own illustration: a gym that sells memberships decides in advance to offer a personal-training starter pack at the moment of signing.
The sale is not over when the customer says yes. It is just starting.
Key tool: Planned add-on and upsell offers
10
The Value-Added Proposition
›
Rather than competing on price, Cardone urges sellers to add things the competition does not: extra service, bonuses, guarantees, or bundled benefits that make your offer worth more than the sticker suggests.

The point is to give the buyer a reason to choose you that has nothing to do with being cheaper.
Compete on what you add, not on what you subtract.
Key concept: Value-added offers over discounts
11
Act Hungry
›
A short chapter with a blunt message: customers respond to sellers who clearly want the business. Cardone says to show urgency, energy, and eagerness instead of the cool detachment many professionals mistake for confidence.

He ties hunger to follow-up and persistence: the hungry seller calls back, shows up, and keeps asking.
Wanting the business, visibly, is not desperate. It is persuasive.
Key attitude: Visible hunger
12
Expand Acceptable Client Profile
›
When business slows, Cardone argues sellers should widen who they are willing to serve. Many companies unknowingly restrict themselves to a narrow ideal customer, ignoring adjacent segments that would buy gladly.

The chapter pushes readers to revisit assumptions about who qualifies and to go after the clients they previously dismissed as too small, too different, or too unlikely.
Reviewer's own illustration: a firm that only serves companies over 100 staff tests one outreach week to firms of 20 to 50.
If you are short on revenue, check how narrowly you have defined a customer.
Key exercise: Widen the target client profile
13
Effective Marketing Campaigns
›
Cardone treats marketing as the force multiplier for sales activity. He argues for consistent, measurable campaigns with a clear offer and call to action, rather than vague brand-building that cannot be tied to revenue.

The emphasis is on staying visible during a downturn when competitors go quiet, and on campaigns that give prospects a specific next step.
Marketing without a clear offer and next step is just noise.
Key practice: Offer-driven campaigns
14
Repackaging for Increased Profits
›
If the current offer is not selling, repackage it. Cardone describes combining products and services, changing how they are bundled, or reframing them for different buyers to raise perceived value and average sale size.

The underlying idea is that the market responds to packaging and positioning as much as to the underlying product.
Sometimes the product is fine and the package is the problem.
Key tool: Bundling and repositioning
15
The Power Schedule to Advance and Conquer
›
Cardone argues that time is the sales professional's most mismanaged asset. The Power Schedule is a structured plan that blocks the day and week around selling activities, with prospecting and client contact protected from interruptions and admin work.

He insists that a calendar full of non-selling activity is the clearest sign that a seller is not serious about results.
Reviewer's own illustration: a manager blocks 8 to 10 each morning for calls only and moves email and admin to the afternoon.
What is in your calendar is what you actually value.
Key tool: The Power Schedule
16
An Advance-and-Conquer Attitude
›
The book's mindset chapter. Cardone argues that attitude determines activity, and activity determines income. He urges readers to stop absorbing negative news and conversation, take ownership of results, and commit fully rather than hedging.

The advance-and-conquer attitude is presented as a daily choice, not a personality trait.
You can't sell offensively with a defensive mindset.
Key concept: Attitude drives activity
17
Your Freedom Financial Plan
›
Cardone connects selling to personal finances. A freedom financial plan means knowing your numbers, controlling expenses, saving, and building toward independence so that fear of money does not drive bad selling decisions.

The chapter argues that sellers with financial breathing room are bolder, more patient, and less likely to panic-discount.
Financial desperation makes you sell worse, not harder.
Key idea: Financial stability as a sales advantage
18
The Most Important Skill Needed to Advance and Conquer
›
The title promises a single essential skill. The sources I could read did not state it, so treat my reading as unverified: it is almost certainly about closing, meaning asking for the business and driving to a decision.

The likely message is to practise it, expect objections and treat them as part of the process.
The ability to ask for the order and handle the objection is the skill beneath all the others.
Key skill: Closing
19
The Unreasonable Attitude
›
In the final chapter, Cardone argues that ambitious results require expectations others call unreasonable: higher targets, more effort, and refusal to accept ordinary limits. Reasonable people produce reasonable outcomes, and in a competitive market those are not enough.

He urges readers to set goals beyond what feels comfortable and then build activity to match.
Reasonable expectations produce average results.
Key attitude: Unreasonable commitment
Conclusion
C
Conclusion: How to Guarantee Your Position
›
Cardone closes by tying the pieces together: activity, follow-up, service, price discipline, and attitude. He argues that market position is not given, it is earned and defended every day by the people willing to out-work and out-serve the competition.

The contents also list an Afterword and a Glossary after the conclusion.
Number one is not a title you win once. It is a position you keep earning.
Key idea: Position is maintained by daily activity

Try it (reviewer's suggestion): write down the one logical add-on for each product you sell.
Value · Ch. 7, 8, 10

Wow Delivery, Price and Added Value

Find small, visible ways to over-deliver, and answer price objections by adding value before cutting the number.

Try it (reviewer's suggestion): before any discount, list three value points you have not yet mentioned.
Market · Ch. 12, 14

Widen the Client Profile and Repackage

Question who you consider an ideal customer and test different bundles of what you already sell.

Try it (reviewer's suggestion): list three customer types you turn away and approach one; combine two offers into a premium package.
Habits · Ch. 11, 15

The Power Schedule and Act Hungry

Block calendar time for selling, protect it from admin, and respond to prospects quickly and visibly eagerly.

Try it (reviewer's suggestion): reserve the best two hours of each morning for prospecting and reply to every inquiry within an hour for a week.

Unreasonable Targets

Set goals well beyond what feels comfortable, then work back to the daily activity they would require.

Try it (reviewer's suggestion): double one monthly target on paper and compute the daily call count it implies.

Dan's Take

Dan rates If You're Not First, You're Last 3 out of 5. He scores the ideas 4, readability 4.5, actionability 3 and evidence 2. He says it is not a book he would have picked up on his own; it came through his book club. He listened to the audiobook and found the narration very readable.

What he took from it is the idea of staying first of mind: if you don't have enough touchpoints for people to think of you first, you do badly in a downturn, and that means being more proactive than is comfortable about reaching out and keeping relationships up. The book prompted a lot of actions, but he found it too salesy and aggressive to adopt as a whole, so actionability loses points.

He disliked the in-your-face tone and the insistence that the reader think the way the author does. He rates the evidence low because it is one person's view of selling, and he found it inconsistent that the author tells readers to ignore science where it doesn't suit his point and then cites science-backed studies where it does. He also noted the author's worldview differs from his own. Overall there are still a fair few points worth taking, mainly on relationship-building and the sales side.

Written up from Dan's own words (12 Nov 2026). His view stands apart from the Claude and reader views above.

Strengths & Limitations

Strengths

Actionable by design: reactivation, follow-up and referral-style asks can start today
Focuses on existing assets (network, past clients, the unsold) before expensive new prospecting
Short, clearly titled chapters that are easy to dip into
Separate chapters on price and on protecting selling time

Limitations

Appears to lean on conviction and anecdote rather than research (unchecked, from published descriptions)
Readers describe repetition and a high-pressure tone
May suit transactional sales better than long, consultative ones (my inference)
Written for the post-2008 economy, so context may feel dated

Who Should Read This

✅
Salespeople in a slow patch who need a push to start calling again.
✅
Owners and managers looking for revenue levers beyond advertising spend.
✅
Service and staffing sellers whose growth depends on re-engaging past clients.
⚠️
Readers who dislike high-pressure sales language will likely find the tone grating.

The Single Most Important Insight

Much of the revenue you need may already sit in your contacts, past clients and open quotes. The book's most useful instruction, going by its chapter order, is to go back to those people, ask directly and keep following up, rather than assuming new leads are the only route to growth. This is Claude's reading of the contents.
