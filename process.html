Process! (BenBella Books, September 2022) is by Mike Paton and Lisa González, with a foreword by Gino Wickman. It argues that a handful of well-chosen, simply documented core processes, actually followed by everyone, are what let a growing company run without the owner holding every answer.

The recurring theme in descriptions of the book is the 20/80 idea: capture the roughly 20% of steps that produce 80% of the result, and do not try to write a manual. The authors also take on the objections founders raise (process is unnatural, takes too long, or kills freedom) before getting into method.

The method itself is short: pick your core processes, document and simplify them, package them where people can use them, then make sure they are followed through training, measurement and supervision. A final part covers common obstacles and building an action plan, with templates and resources at the back.

The Process Framework, In Depth

The book's practical core is two linked tools: a three-phase method to build your processes, and a four-step checklist to make them stick. Each gets the same treatment below. Step 1 of 3

Identify

Decide which handful of workflows define how the company creates and delivers value, and give each a clear name.

What it is

Before writing anything, the leadership team agrees on which processes count as core: the repeatable workflows that make the business what it is. One summary gives a range of roughly 5 to 12; the EOS convention elsewhere is often quoted as 6 to 10, so treat the exact number as a guide, not a rule.

What it looks like and where it goes wrong

Good practice
  • Short list, agreed by the leadership team
  • Precise names everyone uses the same way
  • A single owner for each process
  • Covers the whole path from lead to retained customer
Common failure modes
  • Trying to list every workflow in the company
  • Two leaders using the same name for different things
  • Naming a process after a department, not an outcome
  • Skipping the debate because it feels abstract

Illustrative scenario

Example (mine, not the book's)
A twelve-person design studio lists twenty-three "processes" on a whiteboard. After merging overlaps they end up with six, such as "win the work", "scope the project", "deliver the design", "invoice and collect", "hire and onboard" and "keep the client". The argument about whether scoping belongs inside "win the work" turns out to be the most useful half hour of the day.
Step 2 of 3

Document

Watch how the work is really done, find the snags, and simplify it into a short, clear checklist.

What it is

Per the summaries, you evaluate each core process by observing it firsthand, look for bottlenecks and frustrations, and then simplify into a clear checklist of the major steps. The 20/80 rule governs the level of detail: enough to enforce consistency on what matters, not a manual.

Document, or leave out

Usually worth capturing
  • The major steps everyone must follow
  • Handoffs between people or teams
  • Steps that cause errors when skipped
  • Decision points and who makes them
Usually not worth it
  • Every edge case
  • Basics any competent hire already knows
  • Details that change weekly
  • Anything you are documenting only to feel thorough

Illustrative scenario

Example (mine, not the book's)
The studio's owner thinks "invoice and collect" has four steps. Watching the bookkeeper, the team finds a fifth, unwritten step: she chases late payers on the second Tuesday of the month because that is when the client's finance team approves payments. That step goes on the checklist; the formatting details of the invoice do not.
Step 3 of 3

Package

Put the documented process where people can find it, in a format that suits the task.

What it is

Documentation people cannot find or understand has not helped anyone. The summaries describe choosing a practical format (flowchart, checklist, sequence of images, short video) and placing it where staff can reach it in the moment. The same summary says the book is tool-agnostic in general and warns against buying heavy software before the process itself is clear.

Questions to ask

  1. Where will someone look? At the point of work, not in a folder nobody opens.
  2. Does the format fit the task? A checklist for a routine, a diagram for a handoff, a video for something physical.
  3. Who owns updates? Name a person, or it will go stale.
  4. Has anyone been trained on it? Packaging leads straight into the FBA checklist.
After the method

The FBA Checklist

Four steps that, as summarised, turn a document into a habit: Train, Assess, Supervise, Revise.

The four steps

1. Train
  • Everyone who runs a critical process learns the why, what and how
  • Reinforce over time, because people forget
2. Assess
  • Adherence: are the steps followed?
  • Regularity: is it done often enough?
  • Outcomes: does it deliver the expected result?
3. Supervise (LMA)
  • Leading, Managing, Accountability
  • Clear communication, feedback invited, success reinforced
  • Problem-solving when someone deviates
4. Revise
  • Review each process at least once a year
  • Keep simplifying as conditions change

Illustrative scenario

Example (mine, not the book's)
The studio rolls out its "deliver the design" checklist and trains everyone in one lunch session. Three months later, delivery quality varies again. A quick assessment shows adherence is fine but regularity is not: two project leads skip the mid-project review when busy. The fix is supervision (a weekly check in the leadership meeting), not a rewrite of the document.

The Toolkit

The book's main practical ideas gathered in one place, described from published summaries. The practice plan at the bottom is my own suggestion.

Method

Process Documenter (Identify, Document, Package)

The three-phase build method. Start with a short named list of core processes, simplify each to its major steps, then package it where people will use it. See the Process Framework tab.

Standard

FBA Checklist (Train, Assess, Supervise, Revise)

  • Train everyone who runs a critical process, and reinforce it
  • Assess adherence, regularity and outcomes with a small number of measures
  • Supervise with LMA: Leading, Managing, Accountability
  • Revise at least annually
Rule

20/80 Documentation

Capture the major steps that drive most of the result. If you are debating the fine detail, you have probably gone too deep.

Obstacles

Obstacle to Remedy

  • Fading leader enthusiasm: pick quick, measurable wins first
  • No in-house expertise: build it, hire it, or borrow it
  • Perfectionism: start broad, refine in rounds
  • Tech first: define the process before buying ERP or CRM
  • Leaders not backing the change: champion it and model accountability

A 30-Day Practice Plan

This plan is my own suggestion (the reviewer's), not drawn from the book.
🗓️
Week 1: Leadership team lists its core processes independently, then merges to a short named list with owners.
✍️
Week 2: Pick the most painful one. Watch it being done, then write the major steps on one page.
🎓
Week 3: Put it where the work happens and train the people who run it. Leaders follow it first.
📏
Week 4: Choose one measure for adherence, review it in the weekly meeting, and book the annual revision.

Dan's Take

Dan rates Process! 4.5 out of 5. He really liked it. It helped him see what processes can do, and how to build one so that people understand how complete it needs to be and what to do, and get that across clearly without micromanaging or undermining other people's ability to do things their own way. He rates the ideas 4.

It is a quick book and an easy listen (readability 4), and he has used it as an actionable item (4.5). He isn't sure where it stands on evidence, so he gave it a 3 overall.

His one reservation is that some of the ideas may not be usable for all people in all use cases. But in his current work, where there are many standard operating procedures and processes that need to be followed, a book that shows an effective way to do this, or at least to win maximum buy-in, is very useful.

Strengths & Limitations

Strengths

Focused on one stuck EOS component rather than trying to cover everything
The 20/80 rule counters the perfectionism that stalls most documentation efforts
FBA gives a concrete follow-through checklist, not just a documentation method
Includes templates, formats and a list of obstacles with remedies
Short enough to read in a sitting or two

Limitations

Evidence appears to be experience and case studies, not controlled evidence (I have not checked them)
Written inside the EOS ecosystem, so alternatives are not weighed
Readers already running EOS may find much of it familiar; a reader review calls it basics
Likely light on complex, regulated or heavily technical processes (my inference from its high-level approach)

The Single Most Important Insight

Write down less, and make sure it gets followed. The book's practical bet is that a short, simple, well-named set of core processes, trained, measured and supervised, beats a thorough manual that sits unused.

Overall Summary

Process! looks like a compact, practical guide to the EOS component most teams put off. Its strengths are simplicity and follow-through: the 20/80 rule and the FBA checklist are easy to adopt. Its limits are depth of evidence and its position inside the EOS brand.

If your leadership team keeps deferring process work, it is a reasonable kick-start. Pair it with a critical eye on the case studies, and decide for yourself how much it adds beyond Traction.
