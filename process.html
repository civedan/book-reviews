Process! tackles what Gino Wickman described as the most underutilised component of the Entrepreneurial Operating System. Most leadership teams running EOS understand the idea of the Process component — document your core workflows, get everyone to follow them — but they treat it as an administrative chore rather than a strategic lever. Paton and González set out to change that perception entirely.

The authors' central argument is counterintuitive: the discipline of codifying how your business works is not a constraint on creativity or growth — it is the mechanism that produces both. When your core processes are documented and genuinely followed by every team member, you stop being the bottleneck. Onboarding becomes faster. Quality becomes consistent. The business can scale beyond the knowledge stored in a handful of people's heads. You get your time back.

Paton and González cut through the most common objection head-on: "our business is too complex and dynamic to document." Their response is to reframe what documentation means. You are not writing an ISO manual. You are capturing the 20% of steps that produce 80% of the right results — a handful of essential, high-level waypoints that give any team member a reliable map. The rest is judgment. The 3-Step Process Documenter shows exactly how to do that, and how to get the process out of your head and into practice across the whole organisation.

Part I — The Case for Process
1
Why Process? The Hidden Cost of Running on Tribal Knowledge
›
Paton and González open with a scenario every entrepreneur recognises: a key employee leaves, and suddenly no one knows how to handle their responsibilities. Orders slip. Quality dips. The founder is back in the weeds. This is not a people problem — it is a process problem.

The chapter builds the case that most growing businesses are running on tribal knowledge — unwritten, uncodified, locked inside the heads of a small number of people. That works fine when you're small and everyone can see everything. But it becomes a ceiling as you grow. Consistency suffers, because every person does things differently. Onboarding suffers, because there's nothing to hand new people. Scalability suffers, because the owner or the expert must be consulted on everything.

The authors are careful not to make this feel like a lecture. They acknowledge that most EOS companies have avoided the Process component — not because they're lazy, but because it feels daunting, abstract, or beneath the leadership team's pay grade. This chapter's job is to reframe that: process work is leadership work, because it's the work that ultimately sets the leader free.
Tribal knowledge feels efficient until someone leaves or the business tries to scale. Process documentation is not bureaucracy — it is the foundation of a company that can outlast any individual.
2
The Process Component — Where It Fits in the EOS Model
›
This chapter situates the Process component precisely within the broader EOS model. Paton — a veteran EOS Implementer who co-authored this with González — makes a key observation: most leadership teams who run EOS score themselves highest on Vision and People, and lowest on Process and Data. Yet Process is the component most directly linked to the consistency, scalability, and freedom the owner is actually looking for.

The authors define the Process component with precision: it is the discipline of identifying, documenting, simplifying, and getting the entire organisation to consistently follow the small number of core processes that define how the business operates. Nothing more, nothing less. It is not about documenting every possible task. It is about establishing repeatable, teachable ways of doing the most important things.

A key insight here is that the Process component connects to every other EOS component. When processes are clear, the Scorecard becomes more meaningful (you're measuring the right activities). Rocks get done more reliably (there's a process for how to execute). Issues in the IDS session often trace back to process breakdowns. And new People can be onboarded faster when there's a documented way of working to hand them. Process is not isolated — it is the connective tissue of the operating system.
Process is the component that operationalises everything else. Weak processes mean the other five EOS components operate on a shaky foundation — strong processes amplify the impact of all of them.
3
The Freedom Formula — How Discipline Produces Liberation
›
The chapter that most directly attacks the emotional resistance to process work. The most common objection Paton and González hear from entrepreneurs is some version of: "I didn't start this business to write procedures manuals." The freedom formula is their response.

Their argument: the entrepreneur who refuses to document processes is not operating freely — they are trapped. Every day they spend answering the same questions, solving the same problems, being the single point of failure for the same decisions is a day they are not doing what only they can do. Real freedom — the ability to focus on growth, strategy, and vision — requires that the operational layer of the business can run without constant intervention.

The book draws on Michael Gerber's E-Myth insight but extends it: you are not just building systems so the business can run; you are building systems so the right people can run them. The documented process plus the right person in the right seat equals a business that can grow without the owner being the ceiling. That is the freedom formula: Process + People = Freedom.

The chapter also addresses the "our business is unique" objection. Every business has some creative, dynamic, judgment-intensive work — and that's fine. Process doesn't replace judgment; it handles the repeatable parts so that the people making judgments have more time and bandwidth to make them well.
Discipline in process doesn't restrict freedom — it creates it. The owner who refuses to document processes is not free; they are the single point of failure in every important workflow in their business.
Part II — The 3-Step Process Documenter
4
Step 1 — Identify Your Core Processes
›
The first practical step of the 3-Step Process Documenter: before you document anything, you need to agree on which processes you're actually documenting. Most leadership teams get this wrong by either going too broad (trying to capture every workflow in the business) or too narrow (only documenting one department's processes).

Paton and González define "core processes" as the 6–10 processes that together define how your business creates and delivers value. Most companies have variations on the same set: HR/People, Marketing, Sales, Operations/Fulfilment, Accounting/Finance, and Customer Retention. The names and specific contents vary enormously, but the structure — a small, defined set that covers the end-to-end value chain — is consistent.

The identification exercise is a leadership team activity, not a solo project. Everyone in the room names the processes they believe are core, and the team works toward alignment. This step often surfaces surprising disagreements — what one person thinks is a core process, another sees as a sub-step of something else. Resolving those disagreements is itself valuable, because it forces the leadership team to align on how the business fundamentally works before they start writing anything down.

A practical tool introduced here is the Core Process Identifier: a simple facilitated exercise where leadership team members independently list what they believe are the company's core processes, then compare lists and consolidate toward a mutually agreed set. The goal is to leave the session with a confirmed list of 6–10 named processes and an owner assigned to each.
Name your core processes before you document anything. Six to ten is the right number for most companies — enough to cover how value is created and delivered, few enough to actually complete.
5
Step 2 — Document Your Core Processes (The Right Way)
›
The most substantive chapter in the book, and the one most directly responsible for breaking the "I don't know where to start" paralysis that kills most process documentation efforts.

The authors introduce the 20/80 rule for documentation: you are not writing an exhaustive procedure manual. You are capturing the 20% of steps that produce 80% of the right outcomes. This means a documented process might have as few as 7–10 high-level steps — enough to orient anyone who's new to it, enforce consistency on the things that really matter, and give managers something to hold people accountable to. It does not mean 40 sub-bullets with screenshots and decision trees for every possible edge case.

The documentation session itself is facilitated, not solo. The process owner gathers the 3–4 people who actually do the work — not just the manager, but the practitioner — and facilitates a conversation: "Walk me through how we actually do this, start to finish." What comes out is the real process, not the idealised version. Often the most important discovery is the gap between what leadership thinks the process is and what frontline staff actually do.

Paton and González are deliberate about format: keep it simple. A one-page document with a clear name, a purpose statement, the owner's name, the high-level steps, and any critical decision points. Not a 20-page operations manual. The simpler the document, the more likely it is to be read, trained on, and followed. Complexity is the enemy of adoption.
Document the 20% of steps that drive 80% of the right results — not everything. A one-page process document that gets followed beats a 20-page manual that sits in a drawer.
6
Step 3 — Package and Roll Out Your Processes
›
Documentation is not the finish line — it is the starting line. This is the chapter most process initiatives skip, and it is why most process initiatives fail. A document no one knows about, can find, or has been trained on is worthless.

Packaging means presenting your processes in a format that the whole organisation can access and use. That might be a simple shared drive structure, a process handbook, or a tool like Trainual or Process Street — the authors are tool-agnostic, but they are firm that the format must be accessible, navigable, and actually used by the people who do the work. A process binder on the owner's shelf does not count.

Rolling out means training. Not a one-time all-hands announcement, but genuine individual training: walking each person through the processes relevant to their role, explaining the why behind each step, and getting confirmation of understanding. This step is where leadership commitment shows. If the leadership team skips training or treats it as someone else's job, the organisation will treat the processes as optional — because that's the signal they've been sent.

The chapter introduces the concept of "Process Champions" — designating a specific person in each area who owns the ongoing accuracy and training of their area's processes. This distributes the load and creates accountability for processes being kept current as the business evolves.
Documentation without roll-out is shelf-ware. Assign a Process Champion, train every relevant team member, and put the processes somewhere they can actually find and use them.
Part III — Making It Stick
7
Followed By All — The FBA Standard
›
The chapter that elevates Process from a documentation project to a cultural standard. FBA — Followed By All — is the EOS benchmark for the Process component. It doesn't mean followed by most people most of the time. It means every relevant team member follows the documented process every time — and any deviation is identified, discussed, and either corrected or used to improve the documented process.

Paton and González are direct about why FBA is hard: because leadership often doesn't model it. When the founder routinely skips steps, takes shortcuts, or says "I know the process but in this case I'm going to do it differently," they signal to the entire organisation that the process is optional. FBA starts with the leadership team committing to follow their own processes — not because they're below the leader's pay grade, but because their consistent example is what makes the standard believable.

The chapter addresses the "we hire talented people who don't need process" objection directly. Talented people benefit from process — it frees them from spending mental energy on the repeatable parts of their role, so they can focus their creativity and judgment where it actually matters. The best surgeons follow surgical checklists. The best pilots follow pre-flight procedures. Process doesn't diminish competence; it provides the reliable foundation that lets competence shine.

Accountability for process adherence is built into the EOS rhythm: Scorecard items can measure process compliance, IDS sessions surface process breakdowns as issues, and quarterly performance conversations include process adherence as a real metric. FBA is not aspirational — it is tracked.
FBA means every person, every time — not mostly, not when convenient. Leadership modelling is the single most important variable in whether FBA becomes real or remains a poster on a wall.
8
Process and Onboarding — Your Fastest Path to Consistent Team Performance
›
One of the most immediately tangible payoffs of the Process component: a dramatically better onboarding experience for new team members. When core processes are documented and accessible, you can hand a new person a structured, reliable introduction to how the organisation works — rather than relying on whoever has time to mentor them that week.

Paton and González describe the difference in concrete terms: in a company without documented processes, a new hire's quality depends almost entirely on the quality and availability of whoever onboards them. In a company with documented processes, the baseline is established before the person arrives. The mentor's job shifts from "teach them everything from scratch" to "confirm they understand the documented way, and help them develop judgment within it."

The chapter also covers ongoing process training — not just for new hires, but as a continuous practice. Processes evolve as the business grows, and documented processes must be kept current. Annual process reviews (built into the EOS annual planning rhythm) ensure that what's documented reflects how things are actually done, rather than how they were done two years ago.

There's a practical section on using processes for performance management: when someone isn't meeting expectations, the first question should be "are they following the process?" If the answer is no, the conversation is about accountability. If the answer is yes and performance is still poor, the conversation is about whether the process itself is broken — not just the person.
Documented processes transform onboarding from a lottery into a system. New hires can be productive faster, mentors spend less time, and quality is consistent from day one rather than gradually learned.
9
Common Pitfalls and How to Overcome Them
›
The most candid chapter in the book — an honest catalogue of the ways process initiatives fail, drawn from Paton's years as an EOS Implementer working with hundreds of companies. The five most common pitfalls, each with a concrete remedy:

1. Boiling the ocean. Teams try to document everything at once, get overwhelmed, and abandon the project. Fix: identify your 6–10 core processes, pick the two most urgent ones, and document those first. Done beats perfect.

2. Perfectionism. Endless revision cycles because someone can always think of an edge case that isn't covered. Fix: apply the 20/80 rule firmly. If you're debating step 23 of a 25-step process, you've already gone too deep. Ship it.

3. Documentation without roll-out. Documents get created and filed; no one is trained, no one knows where to find them. Fix: the roll-out phase is non-negotiable. Assign a date, assign a trainer, track completion.

4. Leadership non-compliance. Leaders exempt themselves from following the processes they've created. Fix: leaders go first. The leadership team commits publicly to following the documented processes and models it visibly.

5. Letting processes go stale. A documented process from 18 months ago that no longer reflects reality is worse than no process — it breeds cynicism. Fix: assign ownership, schedule annual reviews, and update processes through the IDS system whenever a deviation reveals an improvement.
The biggest process killer is perfectionism followed by abandonment. Start with two core processes, apply the 20/80 rule ruthlessly, roll them out with real training, and keep them current. That cycle, repeated six times, builds a process-driven culture.
10
The Long Game — Process as a Competitive Advantage
›
The closing chapter zooms out from the mechanics to the strategic picture. Paton and González make an argument that few business books make explicitly: documented, consistently followed processes are a genuine competitive advantage — not just an operational nicety.

Their reasoning: a company that runs on well-documented, consistently followed processes can do three things its competitors cannot. First, it can scale without proportional increases in management overhead — because the process does much of the supervisory work. Second, it can acquire and integrate talent faster — because onboarding is systematic, not ad-hoc. Third, it can improve continuously — because when processes are explicit, failures and inefficiencies become visible and fixable rather than invisible and endemic.

The chapter connects back to the EOS annual planning rhythm: process improvement should be a standing agenda item in the annual plan, with specific Rocks each year to strengthen the most underdeveloped core processes. Over 2–3 years, a company that takes this seriously develops a process maturity that is genuinely hard to replicate.

The final pages return to the freedom theme: the most satisfying outcome of a fully process-driven company is not efficiency metrics or onboarding speed. It is that the owner can take a two-week holiday and nothing breaks. That is not a small thing. For most entrepreneurs, it is exactly why they started the business in the first place.
A company that consistently runs on documented processes has a compounding advantage over time: faster scaling, better talent absorption, and continuous improvement that competitors operating on tribal knowledge simply cannot match.

// What you're documenting — and what you're not

DO document: High-level steps every team member must follow
DO document: Critical decision points and who owns them
DO document: Handoff points between people or teams
DO document: Non-obvious steps that cause errors when skipped

DON'T document: Every possible edge case or exception

Key Concepts & Tools

Standard

FBA — Followed By All

The EOS benchmark for the Process component. Not "mostly followed by most people" — every relevant team member follows the documented process every time. Deviations are surfaced and used either to correct behaviour or to improve the documented process. FBA starts with the leadership team visibly modelling process adherence. When the leader skips steps, everyone watches and learns that the process is optional.

Role

Process Champion

A designated owner for each core process — responsible for keeping the documentation accurate, training new team members, and raising the alarm when the documented process diverges from reality. Process Champions are not a separate department; they are the most senior practitioner in each process area. Ownership without a name attached is no ownership at all.

Exercise

Core Process Identifier

A facilitated leadership team exercise for Step 1 of the 3-Step Documenter. Each person independently lists the core processes they believe run the business, then the team compares lists and consolidates toward 6–10 agreed names. The conflicts and overlaps that surface in this exercise are themselves valuable — they reveal misalignment in how the leadership team understands the business at a fundamental level. Resolving them before documentation begins prevents far worse confusion later.

Concept

The Freedom Formula: Process + People = Freedom

The book's central thesis stated as a formula. The entrepreneur who has the right people in the right seats (EOS People component) AND well-documented processes those people consistently follow is no longer the bottleneck in their own company. Operational decisions can be made without them. Quality is consistent without them. The business can grow without them scaling proportionally. That is the freedom most entrepreneurs are actually looking for when they start a company — and process is the mechanism that delivers it.

Integration

Process in the EOS Rhythm

Process work is not a one-time project — it integrates into the standard EOS quarterly and annual cadence. Process breakdowns become IDS issues in the Level 10 Meeting. Process improvement Rocks are set each quarter. Annual planning includes a dedicated review of which core processes need strengthening in the coming year. The Process component is kept alive by the same accountability structures that drive every other part of EOS.

The Single Most Important Insight

The most transformative idea in Process! is the reframe of what "process discipline" actually means for an entrepreneur. Most founders hear "document your processes" and picture themselves trapped in a bureaucratic cage of their own construction — writing manuals no one reads, enforcing rules that stifle the creativity that made the company good in the first place. Paton and González dismantle this picture completely.

The truth is the inverse: the undocumented business is the cage. When your core processes live only in people's heads, you — the owner — are the only person who can make most important decisions. Every day, you are answering questions that a documented process would have answered for you. Every time a key person leaves, institutional knowledge walks out with them. Every quarter you delay documenting your processes is another quarter where your business is fragile, inconsistent, and dependent on you being available and correct at all times.

The freedom formula — Process + People = Freedom — is not a slogan. It is the logical conclusion of what happens when you stop being the single point of knowledge in your own company. That shift, more than any other, is what allows a business to scale without the owner becoming the ceiling. And it starts not with a massive documentation project, but with a single afternoon of the leadership team naming their six to ten core processes. That first step, modest as it sounds, is the beginning of getting the business out of your head and into the world.

Overall Summary

Process! is exactly what it needs to be: a tight, practical, no-fluff guide to the EOS component that most leadership teams quietly dread. Paton and González understand why the Process component stays undone — the combination of perceived complexity, perfectionism, and the sense that it's administrative rather than strategic — and they dismantle every one of those objections with precision.

The 3-Step Process Documenter is the right tool, at the right level of abstraction. The 20/80 rule removes the perfectionism trap. The FBA standard sets a clear bar that isn't aspirational but operational. And the Freedom Formula reframes the entire exercise from "something we should probably get around to" to "the single most important structural thing we can do to make this business scalable and survivable."

The book's limitations are real but acceptable given its scope. It is deliberately focused — one component of a six-component system — and readers without the Traction context will need to read that book alongside it. It is also lightly tooled, leaving software choices entirely to the reader. But neither of these is a meaningful criticism of what the book sets out to do.

The honest summary: if your EOS company has been stalling on the Process component — if it's been on the quarterly agenda for two years and never actually happened — read this book, then run the Core Process Identifier exercise at your next quarterly. Six months later, you'll wonder why you waited.
