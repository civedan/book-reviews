What This Book Is About

Core Focus
Leadership Transitions
How to take charge in a new role, whether you are promoted, hired from outside, or moved to a new unit
Central Promise
Reach Break-Even Sooner
The break-even point is when the value you have produced matches the value you have consumed; the book is about reaching it faster
Primary Tools
STARS, Five Conversations, Early Wins
Diagnose your situation, negotiate with your boss, and choose early wins with a checklist
Best For
Anyone Starting a Leadership Role
New leaders, newly promoted managers, and the people who design onboarding for them

The First 90 Days by Michael D. Watkins is a structured playbook for the first months in a new leadership role. This is the Updated and Expanded edition (Harvard Business Review Press, the tenth-anniversary edition per the publisher listing at IMD). The cover on this page is that edition.

Its backbone is a set of core challenges, one per chapter: prepare yourself, accelerate your learning, match your strategy to the situation, negotiate success, secure early wins, achieve alignment, build your team, and create alliances, followed by chapters on keeping your balance and on helping everyone else through their own transitions. Almost every chapter ends in a worksheet, table or diagram.

Two diagrams frame the whole book. The break-even chart shows value produced against value consumed over the first months. A pair of cycle diagrams show how inadequate learning, bad decisions and lost credibility feed on each other, while focused learning, informed strategy and early wins build on each other. I rate it more as a working manual than a narrative: it is easy to dip into and harder to read straight through.

Core Ideas at a Glance

⚖️
Every transition has a break-even point. Early on you consume more than you produce. Figure I-1 in the companion PDF plots this against months after entry, and the book is about shortening the distance.
🔁
Two cycles, one choice. The vicious cycle runs from inadequate learning to bad decisions, lost credibility, resistance and weak relationships. The virtuous cycle runs from focused learning to informed strategy, credibility, alliances and early wins.
🧭
Diagnose before you act. STARS (Start-up, Turnaround, Accelerated growth, Realignment, Sustaining success) says the right approach depends on the situation you actually inherited, and most people inherit a mix.
🤝
Negotiate the relationship with your boss. The Five Conversations (situation, expectations, resources, style, personal development) are a structured agenda, and a second table adapts them for your own team.
🏆
Pick early wins deliberately. The book gives an evaluation tool for candidate wins and a FOGLAMP checklist (focus, oversight, goals, leadership, abilities, means, process) for planning them.
🧩
Alignment, team and alliances. Check whether strategy, structure, processes and skills fit; assess the team you inherited; and map who supports, opposes or is undecided.
🧗
Look after yourself. A stress and performance curve, a self-assessment of the core challenges and a map of your advisers address the personal side.
🌊
You are never the only one in transition. The final chapter turns to onboarding and promoting other people, with a Transition Heat Map for spotting where support is needed.

Front Matter and Introduction
I
Preface and Introduction Title not verified
›

This edition opens with a preface for the tenth-anniversary edition, which the publisher listing at IMD describes as adding a view of careers as a series of transitions, updated statistics and new tools. The introduction sets up the book's three framing diagrams.

Verified from the companion PDF: the break-even chart (Figure I-1), the vicious cycle (I-2) and the virtuous cycle (I-3), a Transition Risk Assessment (Table I-1) and a map of key transition milestones (Figure I-4). The risk assessment lists twelve kinds of transition, such as a new industry, a new company, a promotion, leading former peers, moving geographically, doing two jobs at once, or entering an organization already in the middle of major change. You tick the ones that apply and rate each from 1 to 10 for difficulty.

Count how many transitions you are stacking at once before you plan the first 90 days.
Tools: Break-even point · Vicious and virtuous cycles · Transition Risk Assessment · Key transition milestones
Chapters 1 to 4
1
Prepare Yourself Title in book's Table 9-1
›

About the mental shift of leaving the old role behind. Table 9-1 phrases the question as adopting the right mind-set for the new job and letting go of the past.

The tools for this chapter, per the companion PDF: a table of core promotion challenges (broader impact horizon, more complexity and ambiguity, tougher politics, more distance from the front line, more scrutiny, each paired with a response such as delegating more deeply or communicating more formally); a culture pyramid (symbols and language at the visible top, norms in the middle, fundamental assumptions hidden at the base); four onboarding checklists (business orientation, stakeholder connection, expectations alignment, cultural adaptation); and two self-assessments of which kinds of problems you enjoy (technical, political, cultural).

Promotion changes the job in predictable ways; plan for each change rather than doing more of what worked before.
Tools: Core promotion challenges · Culture pyramid · Onboarding checklists · Problem preferences
2
Accelerate Your Learning Title in book's Table 9-1
›

Treats learning as something to plan: what you need to learn, from whom, and how to speed it up. A diagram of sources of knowledge places people such as top management, customers, distributors, suppliers, analysts, salespeople, allies, integrators, historians and builders around the organization's boundary.

A table of structured learning methods covers climate and satisfaction surveys, structured interviews with horizontal or vertical slices of the organization, focus groups, analysis of critical past decisions, process analysis, plant and market tours, and pilot projects, each with what it is good for and which managers it suits.

Choose your learning methods on purpose, and ask everyone the same questions so you can compare answers.
Tools: Sources of knowledge · Structured methods for learning
3
Match Strategy to Situation Title in book's Table 9-1
›

Introduces STARS: start-up, turnaround, accelerated growth, realignment and sustaining success. A table sets out the challenges and opportunities of each. A second table has you list the projects, products or units you own, assign each a STARS situation and allocate your effort across them, then check whether your own preferences are driving the allocation.

A third table contrasts turnarounds and realignments across six steps (organize to learn, define strategic intent, set priorities, build the leadership team, secure early wins, create alliances). A fourth reports survey results on which situation people find most challenging and which they prefer: realignment was named most challenging by 30.3% and start-up most preferred by 47.1%.

There is no universal first-90-days playbook; identify the mix you are in and notice which part you would rather be doing.
Tools: STARS model · Diagnosing your STARS portfolio · Turnarounds versus realignments · Challenges and preferences survey. See the STARS tab for a deeper treatment.
4
Negotiate Success Title in publisher excerpt
›

Focused on the relationship with your new boss. The Five Conversations table gives an agenda: situation (how does your boss see your STARS portfolio), expectations, resources, style, and personal development. For each you record the current status and priorities for the next 30 days.

A second table lists typical roles for your boss by STARS situation (for example, support for tough personnel calls in a turnaround, or constant reality testing in sustaining success), and a third repeats the five conversations for each of your team members.

Treat the boss relationship as something you build deliberately, then run the same agenda downward.
Tools: The five conversations · Matching support to your situation · The five conversations and your team
Chapters 5 to 8
5
Secure Early Wins Title in book's Table 9-1
›

Starts from a waves-of-change diagram: four stages (transition, immersion, reshaping, consolidation) plotted as the intensity of change you institute across about 18 months. A table of problematic behaviour patterns lists symptoms of missing focus, standards, teamwork and urgency; its text did not extract cleanly from the PDF, so I am not summarizing its entries.

The practical tools are an early-wins evaluation tool (four questions scored 0 to 4, so 0 to 16 in total, used to compare candidate focal points and not as a precise measure), the FOGLAMP project checklist (focus, oversight, goals, leadership, abilities, means, process), and a diagnostic flowchart for managing change that asks in turn about awareness, diagnosis, vision, plan and support, with a different action if a step is missing.

Score candidate wins before committing, then plan the chosen one with the checklist.
Tools: Waves of change · Problematic behaviour patterns · Early wins evaluation · FOGLAMP · Diagnostic framework for managing change
6
Achieve Alignment Title in book's Table 9-1
›

Looks at the organization as an architecture: strategic direction, structure, core processes, skill bases and the internal climate and culture, sitting inside an external environment of customers, competitors, suppliers, distributors, investors, media, governments and NGOs.

A process-analysis table sorts activities into production or service-delivery, support, and business processes, using a credit-card business as the worked example, and a process map shows one order passing across customer relations, accounts receivable and fulfilment. The idea, as I read it, is to find where the pieces do not fit together.

A fresh pair of eyes can spot misalignments that insiders have stopped seeing.
Tools: Elements of organizational architecture · Process analysis · Process map
7
Build Your Team Title in book's Table 9-1
›

Links organizational alignment with restructuring the team: assess what is, decide what should be, plan how to get there, then implement. An assessment table has you divide 100 points across six evaluative criteria (competence, judgment, energy, focus, relationships, trust) and mark any that are threshold issues.

A diagram contrasts push tools (incentives, reporting systems, planning processes, procedures) with pull tools (mission statement, shared vision, teamwork). A final table gathers inspirations for vision statements, grouped by what motivates people: commitment to an ideal, great results, contribution, being part of a team, individual growth, control of one's destiny, and trust and integrity.

Weight your criteria before you judge people, and decide which failures disqualify.
Tools: Alignment and team restructuring · Evaluative criteria · Push and pull tools · Inspirations for vision statements
8
Create Coalitions (some listings: Create Alliances) Title varies by source
›

About influence beyond formal authority. Tools: a table for identifying influential players (who, what you need from them, and when); an influence diagram built around a worked example with a fictional manager, showing who supports, who is undecided, who opposes, and where a winning or blocking alliance might form; and a table for the motivations, driving and restraining forces, and perceived alternatives of pivotal players.

A framing-arguments table uses the classical triad: logos (data and reasoned argument), ethos (principles, policies and other rules) and pathos (emotions and meaning), each with questions to ask about the person you want to persuade.

Work out what each pivotal person believes their alternatives are before you argue your case.
Tools: Identifying influential players · Influence diagram · Motivations and alternatives · Framing arguments
Chapters 9 and 10
9
Keep Your Balance Title from summary sites
›

The personal chapter. A Yerkes-Dodson performance curve plots performance against stress, from peak performance to "going over the top" and burnout. An assessment of core challenges turns the eight earlier challenges into diagnostic questions you ask yourself.

A table of adviser types names technical advisers, cultural interpreters and political counselors, and a grid asks you to fill in internal and external names for each. Summary sites describe the chapter as being about disciplined execution and a support system.

Build the adviser grid before you need it, and check honestly which challenges you are neglecting.
Tools: Performance curve · Assessment of core challenges · Types of advisers · Advice-and-counsel network
10
Accelerate Everyone Title from one outline
›

Turns from your own transition to everybody else's. The previous page called this "Expedite Everyone"; I could not confirm that wording, and one study outline gives "Accelerate Everyone". Treat the title as unverified. The companion PDF shows the tools: a Transition Heat Map, a table of reasons transitions fail (some for all transitions, some specific to promotion or onboarding), a comparison of transition coaching and developmental coaching, a diagram linking recruiting and onboarding, and a development grid.

The heat map lists organizational units, notes the major change events in each (rapid growth, turnaround, acquisition) and rates the intensity of onboarding, promotion, geographic moves and lateral moves. The development grid charts the functions you have worked in against the five STARS situations to show blank cells, which the book frames as possible blind spots.

Use the heat map to see where transition risk is concentrated before spreading support evenly.
Tools: Transition Heat Map · Reasons for transition failures · Transition versus developmental coaching · Linking recruiting and onboarding · Development grid

STARS and the Five Conversations, In Depth

The book's central pair of ideas: first diagnose which situation you are in (STARS), then negotiate your boss's support for that situation (the Five Conversations). Situation 1 of 5

Start-Up

Assembling the people, financing and technology to get something off the ground.

What the book's table says

Challenges
  • Building strategy, structures and systems from scratch, with no clear framework or boundaries
  • Recruiting and welding together a high-performing team
  • Making do with limited resources
Opportunities
  • You can do things right from the beginning
  • People are energized by the possibilities
  • There are no rigid preconceptions

Support to ask your boss for

Help getting needed resources quickly, clear measurable goals, guidance at strategic breakpoints, and help staying focused (Table 4-2). The other four situations start from this same list and add to it.

My reading

The risk is not lack of ideas but lack of edges. Without boundaries, every request looks equally urgent, so the boss conversation about goals and focus matters more here than in any other case.

Situation 2 of 5

Turnaround

Saving a business or initiative that everyone agrees is in serious trouble.

What the book's table says

Challenges
  • Reenergizing demoralized employees and other stakeholders
  • Making effective decisions under time pressure
  • Going deep enough with painful cuts and personnel choices
Opportunities
  • Everyone recognizes that change is necessary
  • Affected constituencies offer significant external support
  • A little success goes a long way

Support to ask your boss for

Everything in start-up, plus support for tough personnel calls, for correcting the external image, and for cutting deeply enough and fast enough.

My reading

This is the situation where acting fast is the plan, not the mistake. The failure mode is under-cutting out of kindness and having to do it twice.

Situation 3 of 5

Accelerated Growth

Managing a business that is expanding rapidly.

What the book's table says

Challenges
  • Putting in place structures and systems that permit scaling
  • Integrating many new employees
Opportunities
  • The potential for growth helps to motivate people
  • People are inclined to stretch themselves and those who work for them

Support to ask your boss for

Everything in start-up, plus support for getting investment to fuel growth at the right rate in the right ways, and help making the case for new systems and structures.

My reading

Growth hides weak systems because the numbers keep going up. The case for structure has to be made while things look fine, which is why the boss conversation about investment comes early.

Situation 4 of 5

Realignment

Reenergizing a once-successful organization that now faces problems, often with people unconvinced there is a problem.

What the book's table says

Challenges
  • Convincing employees that change is necessary
  • Carefully restructuring the top team and refocusing the organization
Opportunities
  • The organization has significant pockets of strength
  • People want to continue to see themselves as successful

Support to ask your boss for

Everything in start-up, plus help making the case for change, especially if you are from outside.

Why it matters

In the book's survey (Table 3-4), realignment was the situation most often called the most challenging, at 30.3%, while only 12.7% said they would prefer it. The book contrasts this with start-up: 13.5% called it most challenging and 47.1% most preferred. I do not know the sample or how the survey was run, so treat the percentages as the author's data, not as established fact.

Situation 5 of 5

Sustaining Success

Preserving the vitality of a successful organization and taking it to the next level.

What the book's table says

Challenges
  • Living in the shadow of the former leader and managing the team they created
  • Playing good defense before embarking on too many new initiatives
  • Finding ways to take the business to the next level
Opportunities
  • A strong team may already be in place
  • People are motivated to continue their history of success
  • A foundation for continued success, such as a long product pipeline, may exist

Support to ask your boss for

Everything in start-up, plus constant reality testing (is this really sustaining success, or a realignment in disguise?), support for playing good defense, and help finding ways to reach a new level.

My reading

The trap is mislabelling. A leader who walks into a slowly declining unit and calls it "healthy" is in the wrong playbook, which is why the book has the boss test that label.

The negotiation

The Five Conversations

An agenda for your new boss, repeated for each team member. The book's table records current status and priorities for the next 30 days for each.

The five, with the book's guiding questions

2

3

What resources do you have at your disposal?

4

Style

How can you best work together?

5

Sample follow-up questions (my own, not from the book)

Situation and expectations
  • Which parts of my remit would you call a turnaround, and which healthy?
  • What would a good first 90 days look like to you, in outcomes?
  • What would make you think this is going badly?
Resources, style and development
  • Which of these resources are fixed and which can I make a case for?
  • Do you prefer updates in writing or in person, and how often?
  • When should we first check how I am doing?

Where it goes wrong

Treating it as a single onboarding chat. The table format, with a column for the next 30 days, suggests the book expects you to return to it. Skipping the situation conversation is the costly omission, because everything else depends on the label you agree.

Two points to hold on to. The situations are not interchangeable, and the two that look most alike (turnaround and realignment) call for nearly opposite pacing. And your portfolio is usually a mix, so the real discipline is allocating your effort rather than picking one label.

Diagnostic

Transition Risk Assessment (Table I-1)

Twelve kinds of transition to tick and rate from 1 to 10 for difficulty; the total is your Transition Risk Index. Useful for seeing which dimensions to focus on.

Model

STARS portfolio (Tables 3-1 and 3-2)

List the projects, processes or units you own, assign each a STARS situation, and split 100% of your next-90-days effort across them. The table then asks which situation you most prefer, to check your preferences are not setting your priorities.

Agenda

The Five Conversations (Tables 4-1 to 4-3)

  • Situation, expectations, resources, style, personal development
  • Record current status and priorities for the next 30 days
  • Repeat for each team member
Checklist

FOGLAMP (Table 5-3)

  • Focus: what is the goal for the project?
  • Oversight: how will you oversee it, and who else should take part?
  • Goals: what are the goals, intermediate milestones and timeframes?
  • Leadership: who leads it, and what training do they need?
  • Abilities: what mix of skills and representation is needed?
  • Means: what extra resources, such as facilitation, are needed?
  • Process: are there change or structured processes the team should use?
Scoring

Early Wins Evaluation (Table 5-2)

Four questions scored 0 to 4, giving 0 to 16, to compare candidate wins. The book itself says to use common sense: a zero on the first question outweighs good scores on the rest.

One 1-out-of-5 reader review (a reader blog (ybrikman.com)) found the advice platitudes and obvious points, and the checklists incomplete

Dan's Take

Dan rates The First 90 Days 3 out of 5. He had high hopes for it, but found that many of its concepts amount to almost bulldozing your way through the first 90 days to get as much return on investment as possible, and he doesn't wholeheartedly agree with that.

His biggest takeaway was the people side: getting to know people and understanding who they are, being a team player, and knowing that you are not the most important person on the team, nor the one who understands things the most, so you need to rely on others.

He rates Ideas 3. He struggled to get through it (readability 2.5). Actionability is 3, and he scored Evidence 3 because there are a lot of compelling arguments. His summary: glad he read it, but not one he will go back to in order to stay on top of things. It was good to read once and take the knowledge from it.

Strengths & Limitations

Strengths

Broad coverage as one system: diagnosis, learning, boss, early wins, alignment, team, alliances and self-management
Almost every chapter has a usable worksheet, and the companion PDF collects them
STARS gives a vocabulary for saying "this part needs speed, that part needs patience"
Covers both external hires and promotions, and extends to onboarding others in the final chapter

Limitations

Dense: doing every worksheet in a real first quarter is unrealistic, so you have to prioritize
Evidence is mostly experience and examples, and one survey I could not check
A corporate frame; individual contributors and very small organizations will need to adapt the tools (one critic's view, too)
Many named frameworks can feel like acronym overload on a first read

Who Should Read This

✅
Anyone about to start a leadership role, hired or promoted. Read it before the start date if you can.
✅
Newly promoted managers, for the promotion challenges table and the STARS diagnosis.
✅
HR and talent leaders designing onboarding, who can use the final chapter's heat map and coaching comparison.
⚠️
Individual contributors will get most from the learning and self-management material and should adapt the team and alliance tools.
⚠️
People already months into a role can still use the STARS portfolio and alliance tools, but the early-phase material will matter less.

The Single Most Important Insight

Name the situation before you pick the playbook. STARS turns "be a good leader" into a question you can answer in an afternoon: which of my responsibilities are start-ups, turnarounds, growth, realignments or sustaining success, and which of them do I have a bias toward? Everything else in the book, from the boss agenda to the choice of early wins, changes depending on the answer.

Overall Summary

The First 90 Days is best read as a manual: a set of diagnostics and worksheets organized around the first months of a role. Its strengths are breadth, structure and practicality. Its weaker side is thin independent evidence and a density that rewards dipping in over reading straight through.

Use the whole book once for the map, then return to a chapter's tools when you need them: STARS before you plan, the five conversations before you meet your boss, the early-wins scoring before you commit. Pair it with a critical eye on the survey and case material.
